SetBuddy started because I was tired of guessing.

I'd finish a set in the gym with no real idea if my form was any good. I didn't want a vibe, I wanted the angle. So in June 2025 I built a prototype that could watch me lift. It finds your joints with MediaPipe, turns them into angles, and flags it when your form starts breaking down.

Then it got bigger than one person. 100+ people applied to help, and I hired strictly on qualification, not on who I already knew. Now it's 27 of us across the US and Canada, and honestly a lot of them know their corner of it better than I do. We demoed it at the Congressional App Challenge showcase, including to Rep. Suzanne Bonamici.

It's live on the App Store and Google Play. trysetbuddy.com, or come join the team.

A camera, some math, and a number I can't see myself.

Every project I care about has the same move in it: point a camera at something and get a number out. In the gym, the number is a knee angle. In a lab at OHSU, it's the ratio of a retinal vessel's wall thickness to its opening.

I didn't know anyone at OHSU's Casey Eye Institute. On March 19 I cold emailed Dr. Huang, and that was 7 months, 19 days, 0 hours, 0 minutes, 0 secondsseven months ago. I got his reply at a speech and debate tournament where I narrowly missed qualifying for nationals and ended up as an alternate, which made that loss feel way less personal. I joined the lab in June, and it was supposed to end in August. It's been extended four times now, it runs through at least the start of my senior year, and I was recently promoted to research assistant.

The retina is one of the only places a doctor can look at your blood vessels without surgery, and how thick a vessel's wall is compared to its opening is a marker of cardiovascular disease. Measuring it today takes specialized equipment and a lot of manual work. The project I'm part of, with Dr. Ou Tan and Dr. Huang, is trying to make that measurement automatic.

The goal is a quick vessel check at any doctor's visit, like having your blood pressure taken. Cardiovascular disease killed an estimated 19.8 million people worldwide in 2022, about 32% of all deaths, and 85% of those deaths were heart attacks and strokes. In the U.S., someone dies of it every 34 seconds. The data's unpublished, so there are no real scans on this page. Play with an invented vessel instead.
